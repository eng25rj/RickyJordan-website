The pipeline

Where a part number goes, and who has to trust it.

01 — Design

It starts as geometry.

CAD files, assemblies, and the first part numbers. Get the numbering and the vault structure wrong here and every downstream system inherits it.

02 — Release

Then it becomes a promise.

A release turns a working file into a controlled document. Approvals, effectivity, and a baseline the whole program can point at.

03 — Change

Then somebody redlines it.

Change requests, impact review, and the revision bump. This is where most systems quietly lose the thread between what was approved and what got built.

04 — Build

Then the floor builds to it.

ERP and MES pull the bill of materials, the routings, and the effectivity dates. If PLM and ERP disagree, the floor finds out the expensive way.

05 — Sustain

Then it stays true for years.

Audits, as-built configuration, and the long tail of sustainment — where good configuration management is the difference between an afternoon and a program-level problem.

Track record

Approach

An engineer who likes to know why it works.

It started in mechanical design engineering — years at the CAD workstation, close enough to the hardware to know exactly where a process breaks down when nobody's watching. He's the kind of engineer who takes the system apart to see why it works, then rebuilds it so it can't quietly fail.

From 2012 he wrote one of the CAD community's staple blogs, right here at rickyjordan.com — launch coverage, conference reporting, and a running argument about where CAD should go next. Then he crossed to the other side of the data: running configuration and change for programs where a wrong revision isn't a typo, it's hardware.

The best configuration system is the one engineers stop noticing — because it never gives them a reason to work around it.R. Jordan
